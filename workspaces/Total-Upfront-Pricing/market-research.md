# Market Research: Total Upfront Pricing (Accommodations)

## 🔍 Competitive Landscape
- **Leading OTAs are moving toward upfront total pricing.** Expedia positions “transparency in travel pricing” as a standard and displays bold upfront totals that include mandatory fees and many taxes for hotel stays. (https://www.expedia.com/newsroom/transparency-in-travel-pricing-a-win-for-consumers-and-a-standard-at-expedia)  
- **Implementations vary across major platforms.** Skift reports that Expedia, Airbnb, and Booking.com each adjusted their UIs to comply with California’s SB 478 junk‑fee law but differ on whether taxes appear in the headline price, implying multiple “total price” patterns in the market. (https://skift.com/2024/07/03/expedia-airbnb-and-booking-comply-with-california-junk-fee-law-but-in-different-ways/)  
- **Regulatory guidance flows into OTA partner APIs.** Booking.com’s FTC‑compliance documentation instructs partners serving U.S. travelers to highlight a “total price” including all mandatory charges more prominently than any partial price, pushing affiliates and white‑labels toward total‑upfront displays. (https://developers.booking.com/demand/docs/compliance/ftc-compliance)  
- **Hotels acknowledge feasibility but worry about competitiveness.** HospitalityNet commentary notes that many hotels fear honest‑pricing laws will make them look more expensive on metasearch, even while recognizing that folding mandatory fees into a single advertised rate is technically straightforward when systems expose structured fee data. (https://www.hospitalitynet.org/opinion/4121924.html)  

## 📈 Consumer Sentiment
- **Hidden fees are a major driver of abandonment.** A 2023 TravelBoom survey of 300 U.S. leisure travelers found that 31.5% abandon a booking when they encounter hidden fees, and nearly half say they are more likely to choose hotels with transparent pricing. (https://www.travelboommarketing.com/blog/the-hidden-danger-of-hidden-hotel-fees)  
- **Travelers strongly prefer resort fees in the first price they see.** A national poll reported by USA Today found around 80% of respondents want resort fees included in the advertised nightly rate so they can compare hotels accurately. (https://www.usatoday.com/story/travel/2015/11/02/survey-travelers-hotel-resort-mandatory-fees/74889978/)  
- **Federal regulators explicitly cite consumer frustration with hidden fees.** The FTC’s 2024 announcement of its Junk Fees Rule references widespread complaints about hotels and short‑term rentals obscuring mandatory fees until late in the funnel and estimates the rule will save consumers 53 million hours per year in price‑discovery effort. (https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/12/federal-trade-commission-announces-bipartisan-rule-banning-junk-ticket-hotel-fees)  
- **Press coverage reinforces “all‑in pricing” as the norm.** USA Today frames the FTC rule as bringing hotel and short‑term rental pricing in line with what travelers already expect—a single, all‑inclusive total—highlighting reputational risk for channels that continue drip‑pricing practices. (https://www.usatoday.com/story/travel/news/2024/12/18/new-ban-hotel-hidden-fees/77072038007/)  

## ⚖️ Regulatory Trends
- **U.S. federal – FTC Junk Fees Rule.** The FTC’s Rule on Unfair or Deceptive Fees (finalized December 2024) requires short‑term lodging providers and intermediaries to display the total price upfront, including all mandatory fees such as resort or destination charges, while allowing taxes and optional services to be itemized separately if clearly disclosed before payment. (https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/12/federal-trade-commission-announces-bipartisan-rule-banning-junk-ticket-hotel-fees, https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/rule-unfair-or-deceptive-fees-frequently-asked-questions)  
- **California – SB 478 “Honest Pricing Law.”** Guidance from the California Attorney General explains that, from July 1, 2024, businesses may not advertise or display prices that exclude mandatory fees, so hotels and short‑term rentals must incorporate required fees into the first advertised price, excluding only government‑imposed taxes and certain shipping costs. (https://oag.ca.gov/node/585017)  
- **Colorado – HB25‑1090 junk‑fee ban.** Colorado’s 2025 law treats advertising prices that omit mandatory fees for goods, services, or property as a deceptive trade practice and, according to legal commentary, specifically targets resort‑fee and service‑fee structures in hospitality, requiring all‑in advertised prices subject to limited exceptions. (https://leg.colorado.gov/bills/hb25-1090, https://www.jdsupra.com/legalnews/what-is-colorado-s-junk-fee-ban-1964668/)  
- **European Union – Omnibus Directive on price transparency.** The Omnibus Directive (2019/2161) amends EU consumer law to tighten price‑transparency rules, including requirements that advertised price reductions reference the lowest price in the prior 30 days and that traders avoid misleading base prices, increasing scrutiny of artificially low “from” rates in accommodation marketing. (https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/en/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32019L2161)  
- **Cross‑channel enforcement focus.** FTC guidance states the Junk Fees Rule applies to any business “offering, displaying, or advertising” covered services, and California guidance stresses that honest‑pricing obligations apply across websites, apps, and marketing, signaling that OTAs and white‑label engines are in scope alongside brand sites. (https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/rule-unfair-or-deceptive-fees-frequently-asked-questions, https://oag.ca.gov/node/585017)  

## 💡 Market Risks / Gaps
- **Jurisdiction complexity risks inconsistent or non‑compliant pricing.** Differing requirements in the U.S. federal rule, California SB 478, Colorado HB25‑1090, and EU Omnibus law mean static fee‑display rules or property‑supplied descriptions can easily produce non‑compliant partial prices for certain user or property locations. (https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/rule-unfair-or-deceptive-fees-frequently-asked-questions, https://oag.ca.gov/node/585017, https://leg.colorado.gov/bills/hb25-1090, https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/en/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32019L2161)  
- **Data‑quality gaps on mandatory fees.** Commentary around California’s law highlights that many properties still model resort and mandatory fees as loosely structured surcharges, making it difficult for OTAs to compute reliable totals without custom mapping or rules, which can create discrepancies across channels. (https://www.hospitalitynet.org/opinion/4121924.html)  
- **No consistent industry definition of “total price.”** Reporting on California compliance shows major OTAs diverging on whether taxes are included in headline prices and how included fees are labeled, leaving consumers with inconsistent expectations and complicating standardized engineering logic for global deployments. (https://skift.com/2024/07/03/expedia-airbnb-and-booking-comply-with-california-junk-fee-law-but-in-different-ways/)  
- **Limited research on white‑label experiences.** TravelBoom’s 2023 survey focuses on direct hotel booking journeys, suggesting a gap in published benchmarks for how travelers perceive transparency in white‑label or affiliate booking flows, which a white‑label OTA provider could address via proprietary testing and research. (https://www.travelboommarketing.com/blog/the-hidden-danger-of-hidden-hotel-fees)
